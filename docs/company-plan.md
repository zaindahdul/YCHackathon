# Formshop — an agent-run company that makes useful branded objects

Working name only; availability and trademark status have not been checked.

## The idea in one sentence

**Tell us what your startup needs on its desk, at its booth, or inside its prototype; our agent-run shop designs the object, checks it, prices it, and manages the job through delivery.**

Start with branded functional desk objects for startups. The business sells completed custom design jobs and, when a supplier is confirmed, manufacturing coordination. CAD is the production engine; merchandise is the first customer acquisition channel. This is one company with a narrow starting catalog and a path into noncritical custom parts.

The strongest version of the pitch is: “A founder gives our company a logo and a job to do. It turns that into a useful physical product and runs the order.”

## The ambitious version: an autonomous microfactory

“Turn your startup into a product.” A company supplies its identity and the job its customers or team need done. The shop proposes something distinctive, engineers it within supported constraints, sells the job, and coordinates production. A voice-agent startup might want a branded phone/QR display; a developer-tools company a terminal-inspired cable organizer; a hardware team a dimensioned demo cradle. These are examples to validate, not established orders.

The long-term company is a physical-product studio with no founder coordinating every brief and supplier email. Custom merch is the accessible first market; useful fixtures and custom noncritical parts make the larger vision credible. The weekend demonstration should compress that vision into one genuinely completed job. Brand character can change the text, relief, color/insert and proportions without abandoning a tested structural template.

## Why this wedge

Srujan's original idea combines custom CAD parts and custom merch for YC companies. Generic engineering parts and clothing pull in different directions: one needs dimensional trust, the other needs print-on-demand logistics. Useful branded objects connect them. A QR display, phone stand, or cable dock has geometry, visible branding, and a clear buyer. Nearby founders can evaluate the product immediately.

This is a hypothesis to test, not proven demand. A custom-object business could be low-frequency, price-sensitive, and operationally messy. The weekend should establish whether customers value the outcome enough to pay and whether agents can deliver it reliably.

## First customer and first offer

**Customer:** a small startup preparing for demos, recruiting, a launch, or a booth. Start with founders who voluntarily opt in at this event; YC is an initial audience, not an implied affiliation or partner.

**First paid deliverable:** an approved, dimensioned 3D-printable design package for a custom branded desk display, including source/STEP where supported, STL, preview, dimensions, material recommendation, and print notes. Physical production is a separately confirmed service. If nobody can print by Sunday, the digital product still has an explicit, complete deliverable.

**First three product families, in order:**

1. **Branded QR desk display:** rounded base, text/logo face, flat area for an external printed QR insert, customer-selected size. Use a tested QR sticker/insert first; tiny embossed QR geometry introduces unnecessary scan risk. Destination must be customer-approved and scan-tested on a real phone.
2. **Phone stand with branding:** constrained slot and angle, cable opening, tested dimensions for the stated device/case. A preview alone does not prove fit or stability; label unprinted versions as unverified prototypes.
3. **Desk organizer/cable dock:** dimensioned pockets/channels from measured requirements. No claim of precise press fit until tolerances and fabrication are tested.

**Build only #1 end to end first.** #2 and #3 are expansion candidates, not equal weekend commitments. An attendee with an urgent bracket request can be interviewed without adding an arbitrary-CAD feature.

## The customer journey

1. The founder submits company name, a logo they can authorize, what the object must do, dimensions or a supported preset, quantity, target date, budget, and contact preference. A URL can provide context but does not replace permission to use assets.
2. The sales agent asks only missing questions needed to produce a safe, scoped brief. It names what is unknown rather than inventing dimensions.
3. The design agent produces one design and a rendered preview, then the QA role checks geometry and export evidence.
4. The customer sees a dimensioned proposal, included revision count, digital delivery scope, and a separately labeled manufacturing estimate if available.
5. The customer requests a revision or approves the exact design version. Changes invalidate prior approval and quote when costs change.
6. For a digital sale, verified payment triggers a download package and delivery record. For physical production, approved design, verified payment, confirmed supplier quote, and spending policy must all be satisfied before submission.
7. Operations tracks supplier acknowledgment and status, handles failures, and asks for feedback. Reorders reuse the approved specification with a fresh quote.

Customer approval of what they are buying is normal commerce. Founder intervention means Srujan or Zain had to change prompts/code, correct an artifact, operate a supplier interface, or resolve an exception manually. Count these separately.

## What makes this an autonomous company

The business loop is **demand → brief → design → QA → quote → approval → payment → delivery → feedback → next offer**. An agent CEO prioritizes work and receives exceptions; specialist agents execute bounded tasks. Deterministic software owns payment truth, transitions, duplicate prevention, and spending limits.

Autonomy is not the number of agents. A rejected unsuitable job, a corrected design, a recovered supplier error, and a fulfilled approved order are stronger evidence than five agents describing their intentions.

## Roles

| Role | Owns | Decision or artifact | Hard boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company operator / sales | Intake and qualification | Structured brief, one clarification, scoped proposal | No unsolicited outbound launch without authorization |
| Product designer | Parameterized design | CAD source, previews, versioned export | Supported product family and dimensional bounds |
| QA / production | Geometry and manufacturability review | Pass/fail with actual tool results | Cannot certify structural safety or physical fit from a render |
| Operations / finance | Quote, approvals, delivery, reconciliation | Cost ledger, delivery package, supplier status | No purchase without all preconditions and configured spend authority |

Begin with two or three actual Naïve agents if four adds orchestration overhead; keep QA independent of the designer's unsupported claims. Expose the roles as working units, not pretend employees with fake activity.

## Pricing and economics — experiments, not supplier quotes

Test a **$29–$49 digital design package** for one supported object with one revision. Test a **$99–$199 small-batch physical job** only after receiving a real supplier quote. These are proposed validation ranges, not asserted market prices. Do not combine customer revenue, sponsor credits, and test transactions.

Illustrative physical job: ten small pieces sold for $149. Assume fabrication $50, finishing/packing $10, shipping $12, payment fees $5, model/tool costs $5, and a $10 reprint reserve. Contribution is $57 (38.3%) **before founder labor, tax, overhead, and acquisition**. This example is not a quote and assumes subsidized human handling; replace every cost with evidence. A $30 manual handoff would reduce it to $27. Do not promise this margin in the pitch.

For a digital package, record generation and revision costs plus human exception time. A $39 sale with $4 model/tool costs and a $5 revision reserve leaves $30 before fees, labor, overhead, and tax. If founder repair dominates, autonomy and economics both fail.

The sales agent should decline or reprice jobs below a configured contribution floor rather than silently accept loss-making complexity. Customer-visible prices must come from a real rate card or confirmed quote, not improvised language-model arithmetic.

## Distribution and validation

The [short-window acquisition plan](customer-acquisition.md) is the operational marketing playbook. It tests the actual job, buyer and fabrication route before treating free previews as demand. Use $39 as the first concrete digital-package price experiment inside the range above; this is not a physical-product price.

Before adding features, talk to five opt-in founders. Ask what event/desk item they last bought, what was painful, who approved it, the quantity, the deadline, and whether they would buy this specific deliverable at the displayed price. Log exact reactions and permission to follow up; do not report conversations as sales.

Offer a real brief at the event using a QR link or in-person invitation. Let the agents handle the next steps through an explicitly authorized channel. Sample designs can be labeled samples. Customer logos, testimonials, revenue, and delivery photos require actual permission and evidence.

A useful weekend target is three independent briefs, one approved design, and one genuine paid deliverable if account setup permits. These are validation goals, not a condition for pretending success. If customers will not pay for a design file, learn whether physical delivery is essential before claiming the digital fallback validates the original proposition.

## Why this could become bigger

The initial wedge teaches the company how to convert an underspecified request into a validated spec, select a process/supplier, manage revisions, and close the order. With demonstrated reliability, expand into repeat office fixtures, retail displays, device housings, and noncritical replacement parts. Over time the asset is the proven specification, revision, supplier, cost, and outcome history—not prompts or a commodity CAD model.

A “unicorn” argument requires evidence of repeat demand, transferable quality checks, attractive economics, and a large expansion market. A weekend cannot establish those. YC startups alone are not the long-term market.

## Scope boundaries for the weekend

- No arbitrary mechanical engineering, safety-critical parts, food-contact certification, electrical claims, or guaranteed tolerance/strength.
- No clothing catalog, marketplace, supplier bidding platform, inventory system, social autoposting campaign, or ten-agent hierarchy.
- No delivery-date promises until a supplier accepts the exact job and date.
- Conventional shirts/totes can come later through a fulfillment API; they do not belong in the first vertical slice.
- Best Design / People's Choice are plausible benefits of a compelling object and clear experience. Add mobile automation only when it removes an actual operational bottleneck.

## Kill or change the idea if

Founders like previews but will not buy the output; accepted orders require constant human CAD repair; no supported product has defensible quality checks; manufacturing coordination costs more than its margin; or adding more product families repeatedly breaks delivery. Narrowing the catalog, adjusting the offer, or selling design services is an explicit business decision to measure, not hide.
